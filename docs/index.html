Stepper BLE Controller

How a foot pedal, an ESP32 and a phone turn a motor

An ESP32 sends a stream of electrical pulses to a motor driver. Each pulse moves the motor one small step. This page shows what those pulses look like, what "pulse rate" and "duty cycle" mean, and how the firmware decides when to send them.

The ESP32 does not power the motor. It only tells the driver when to step. The driver supplies the current. The phone is optional: the pedal and counter work without it.

2 · Pulses

One pulse, one step

A pulse is the signal going from LOW (0 V) to HIGH (3.3 V) and back. The driver watches for the rising edge, the moment the signal jumps up, and moves the motor one step each time it sees one.

A common stepper motor needs 200 steps for one full turn, so each step is 1.8°. To turn the motor faster, send the pulses closer together.

3 · Pulse rate

Pulse rate (frequency) sets the speed

Pulse rate is the number of pulses per second, measured in hertz (Hz). 500 Hz means 500 pulses, so 500 steps, every second. This firmware allows 100 to 1000 Hz and starts at 500 Hz.

The window is always 20 ms wide. Move the slider and count how many pulses fit.

period = 1 ÷ frequency and RPM = frequency × 60 ÷ steps per revolution. The steps-per-revolution figure depends on the motor and on the microstep switches on the driver, so check the real hardware.
4 · Duty cycle

Duty cycle is how long each pulse stays HIGH

Duty cycle is the share of each period that the signal is HIGH, as a percentage. At 50% the signal is HIGH for half the period and LOW for the other half.

1.00 msLOW time per pulse
500rising edges per second
Duty cycle does not change the motor speed. Slide it between 5% and 95%: the rising edges stay in the same places, so the step count is the same. Only at 0% (always LOW) or 100% (always HIGH) do the edges disappear and the motor stops.

This firmware uses exactly two duty values: 512 out of 1024 (50%) to run, and 0 (0%) to stop. The driver only needs each pulse to be a few microseconds wide, and 50% gives 0.5 to 5 ms, so there is a wide margin.

5 · Inside the ESP32

A counter and a comparison make the pulses

The pulses come from a hardware block called LEDC. Software does not toggle the pin, so Bluetooth activity cannot disturb the timing. LEDC has two parts:

A counter that climbs from 0 to 1023 and starts again, over and over. One full climb is one period. A compare value (the "duty" value). The pin is HIGH while the counter is below it and LOW once the counter passes it.

The counter always wins: once it reports "complete", nothing can run the motor. If the phone disconnects, the firmware returns to Auto, so the motor cannot be left forced on.

Reaction time. The firmware checks the pedal and counter every 50 ms. In the worst case the motor keeps stepping for 50 ms after the counter completes: at 500 Hz that is up to 25 extra steps.

8 · Speed limits

How fast could the ESP32 pulse?

The 1000 Hz ceiling is a setting in this firmware, not a limit of the chip. The chip's limit follows from the counter in section 5: a counter with more steps takes longer to complete one climb.

maximum frequency = clock ÷ 2resolution bits, with an 80 MHz clock.
Counter resolutionMax pulse rateLog scale

A stepper or servo input only needs a 50% pulse, so the resolution can be lowered to reach higher rates. In practice the driver and the motor set the limit long before the ESP32 does.

9 · Driving a servo

Is this pulse rate enough for an industrial servo?

Many industrial servo drives accept the same kind of pulse train ("pulse and direction" position mode). The difference is resolution: a stepper needs 200 pulses per turn, while a servo drive typically expects thousands. The exact number is a drive parameter, usually called the electronic gear ratio.
