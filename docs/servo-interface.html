Connecting an ESP32 to a 5 V servo drive

The ESP32's signal pins work at 3.3 V. A servo drive's pulse input expects 5 V. A small buffer chip between them fixes the mismatch. This page shows why it is needed, what it costs, and how to wire it.

1 · Two kinds of pin

The board has a 5 V pin, but it is not a signal

An ESP32-S3 development board carries both voltages. They do different jobs.

The 5 V pin: power

  • It is the USB supply passed straight through.
  • It is always on. The firmware cannot switch it.
  • Use it to power the buffer chip.

The GPIO pins: signals

  • These are the pins the firmware can pulse.
  • They swing between 0 V and 3.3 V, because the chip itself runs on 3.3 V.
  • They must never receive 5 V. It can damage the chip.

So the pulses that leave the ESP32 are always 3.3 V pulses, whatever the board's 5 V pin offers.

2 · Why 3.3 V falls short

The drive's input is a small light, and it needs enough current

Most servo drives receive the pulse through an opto-isolator: a tiny LED inside the drive that shines on a sensor. The LED sits behind a resistor chosen for a 5 V signal. Feed it 3.3 V and less current flows, so the LED is dimmer and the drive may miss pulses.

3 · The buffer

A buffer copies the signal at a higher voltage

A buffer chip has inputs and outputs. Whatever logic level arrives at an input, it repeats on the matching output, using its own supply voltage. Power it from 5 V, feed it 3.3 V pulses, and it produces the same pulses at 5 V.

One chip has four to eight channels, so a single chip carries the pulse and direction signals for all three axes.

4 · Which chip family

The buffer must accept 3.3 V as a clear "high"

Not every 5 V buffer understands a 3.3 V input. Each chip family has a threshold: the lowest voltage it is guaranteed to read as HIGH. The ESP32's HIGH must sit above that threshold.

Speed and power: nothing you could measure on the machine

6 · Wiring

One channel, drawn out

This shows the pulse signal for one axis. The direction signal, and the other axes, repeat the same pattern on the chip's other channels.

3.3 V signal5 V power and 5 V signalground
  1. Power the buffer from the 5 V pin and join its ground to the ESP32's ground. Without a shared ground the two sides have no common reference and the signal is meaningless.
  2. Fit a 10 kΩ pull-down resistor from each buffer input to ground. Section 7 explains why.
  3. Fit a 100 nF capacitor between the buffer's VCC and ground pins, as close to the chip as possible. It absorbs the small current spikes the chip makes when it switches.
  4. Tie the output-enable pin (OE) to ground so the outputs are always active, and tie any unused inputs to ground so they do not float.
  5. Keep the wires to the drive short, and run each signal twisted together with its ground return, away from the motor power cables.

Which way round at the drive

The diagram drives PULS+ from the buffer and returns PULS− to ground, so the input LED lights when the buffer output is HIGH. Some drives are documented the other way: PULS+ to 5 V and PULS− to the signal, so the LED lights on LOW. Both work with this buffer; follow the drive's manual, and set the drive's pulse-polarity parameter to match.

The better option: a wire pair per signal

Drives usually label their fast 5 V inputs "line driver" inputs. They are designed for a differential signal: two wires that always carry opposite levels. Noise picked up along the cable hits both wires equally and cancels out. An AM26C31 chip, powered from 5 V, converts each ESP32 pin into such a pair. For a machine with three servo drives and long cables this is the more robust choice, and it is what lets the drive accept its highest pulse rates.

7 · Power-up

Why the pull-down resistors matter

For a short time after power-on or reset, the ESP32's firmware is not running yet and its pins are not driven. An undriven wire "floats": it drifts and picks up noise. The buffer faithfully amplifies that noise into 5 V pulses, and the drive may move the motor.

A pull-down resistor holds the wire at 0 V whenever nothing else is driving it. Once the firmware starts, the pin easily overrides the resistor.

Also keep the drives' servo-on (enable) signal off until the firmware has started and homed the machine. Then stray pulses during power-up cannot cause motion even if they occur.
8 · Signals coming back

The other direction needs protection too

The buffer only carries signals out of the ESP32. Signals coming in, such as a drive's alarm or in-position output, or a limit switch, are usually 24 V on an industrial machine. They must not reach an ESP32 pin directly.

Incoming signalHow to bring it in safely
Drive alarm, ready, in-position outputsThrough an opto-isolator module: 24 V on one side, a 3.3 V signal on the ESP32 side.
Limit and home switchesThe same opto-isolator modules, or switches wired to 3.3 V only, with short shielded cable.
Emergency stopWired directly to the drives and spindle power. The ESP32 may read it, but must not be the thing that enforces it.

The buffer itself does not isolate the ESP32 from the drive. The drive's opto-isolated input does that job. Confirm in the manual that the pulse inputs are opto-isolated.

Every voltage, current and threshold on this page is a typical value. Before wiring, read the pulse-input section of the manual for the actual servo drive and the datasheet of the actual buffer chip.
